Early-Warning Credit Risk: Setting the Threshold a Bank Can Live With

A 1-year-ahead corporate bankruptcy risk model, where the real BA work was defining what "risky enough to flag" means in business terms — not the model architecture.

RoleBA + Builder
DomainCorporate Credit Risk
StackXGBoost · MLP · SMOTE/CTGAN
Data78,682 firm-year obs., 1999–2018

The business problem

A credit or risk team needs advance warning before a corporate borrower defaults — but bankruptcy is rare relative to the full portfolio, so a model optimized purely for accuracy will happily predict "not distressed" for everyone and still look accurate. The business requirement isn't "build a classifier" — it's "tell us, at a cost we can defend, which loans to review before it's too late," which means the decision threshold has to be set by the cost of a missed default, not by a default 0.5 cutoff.

BA artifacts produced

Requirements definitionModel must flag distress risk one year in advance using financial statement data already collected in normal reporting cycles — no new data source dependency, so the tool is usable without a systems change request.
Decision-threshold policyThreshold tuned explicitly around the business asymmetry: a missed default (false negative) costs materially more than a false alarm that triggers an analyst review. Ablation studies were run specifically to justify where that line sits, rather than defaulting to standard classifier cutoffs.
Stakeholder framingPositioned as a portfolio-review prioritization tool for Risk and Credit teams, not an automated approval/denial system — output routes a subset of the portfolio to human review rather than replacing underwriting judgment.
Model selection rationaleCompared XGBoost and MLP across raw features vs. engineered financial ratios, and evaluated three imbalance-handling strategies (class weighting, SMOTE, CTGAN synthetic augmentation) to document why the simpler, more interpretable approach was recommended over a more complex one.

Outcome

A tuned XGBoost model on a carefully selected set of original financial features outperformed the more complex data-augmentation approaches — a useful finding in itself: more synthetic data isn't automatically a better answer, and saying so kept the recommended solution simpler to explain to non-technical stakeholders.
